## Quick Answer

At the 35% APR cap, stretching a $5,000 loan from 12 months to 60 months cuts the monthly payment from about **$500 to about $177** — and raises total interest from about **$996 to about $5,648**. At five years you repay more in interest than you borrowed. The term is the negotiable number, and prepayment rights are the lever almost nobody asks about.

The mistake is not choosing a long term — it is choosing one without knowing it costs $3,000 more.

## $5000 Personal Loan Bad Credit Canada: Why the Rate Isn't the Variable

Since **1 January 2025** the criminal interest rate has been **35% APR**, with exemptions set out in the *Criminal Interest Rate Regulations* published in the *Canada Gazette, Part II* on 19 June 2024. The regulations apply prospectively, to agreements entered into on or after that date.

For a $5,000 loan that has a specific consequence: it is far above the $1,500 payday ceiling, so there is no exemption available and 35% APR is the hard maximum.

It also explains why published rate ranges look inconsistent. Comparison pages still quote lender ranges topping out at 39.99% or 46.96%, because those describe entire loan books including pre-2025 agreements. goeasy's own first-quarter FY2026 disclosure reported that **86.6% of total gross consumer loans receivable carried a rate at or below the 35% maximum applicable to new loans** — the balance being legacy contracts.

So the rate conversation has narrowed to a single check: **a new agreement should not exceed 35% APR.** Everything else that determines what you pay is structural.

## $5000 Personal Loan Bad Credit Canada: Six Term Traps

**1. Being quoted a payment instead of a total.** "$195 a month" sounds manageable. "$9,353 repaid on $5,000 borrowed" is the same deal described honestly. Always ask for the **total cost of borrowing in dollars** — lenders are required to disclose it.

**2. Accepting the default term.** Application forms frequently pre-select a long term because it maximises approval and revenue. It is a field you can change.

**3. No prepayment plan.** At 35% APR, paying six months early on a 48-month loan saves real money. But the right to do so is not automatic — ask whether extra payments reduce **principal**, whether lump sums are allowed, and whether a fee applies. Get it in writing.

**4. The top-up refinance.** Six months in, a lender may offer to increase the loan. That usually means refinancing the balance into a new, longer agreement and **restarting the amortisation**. The payment can look unchanged while the total interest climbs by thousands. Ask for the new total cost in dollars before agreeing.

**5. Optional insurance presented as standard.** Loan protection insurance is a separate product with its own premium, frequently added by default. It is optional. Price the loan without it first, then decide.

**6. Ignoring security.** At $5,000 the secured-versus-unsecured gap becomes material. Fairstone, for instance, publishes a lower rate range on secured lending than on unsecured. Security genuinely reduces the price because it reduces the lender's loss on default — which is also exactly why it is a serious decision. The asset can be taken.

## The Prepayment Arithmetic Nobody Runs

This is the most useful thing you can do with a $5,000 loan at a capped rate, and it takes ten minutes.

Take a 48-month loan at 35% APR: roughly $195 a month, about $4,353 in interest.

Now add **$50 a month** to every payment. You are paying $245 instead of $195 — an amount most budgets can find — and the loan clears in roughly 33 months instead of 48. Total interest falls to approximately **$2,850**, a saving in the region of **$1,500**.

The mechanism is simple: at a high rate, most of an early payment is interest, so anything extra attacks the principal that is generating it. The effect compounds every month.

Two conditions make it work, and both must be confirmed before you sign:

- Extra payments must go to **principal**, not be held as a prepayment of the next instalment.
- There must be **no penalty** for paying ahead.

If a lender will not confirm both in the written agreement, that is a reason to compare another offer.

## The Disclosure You Are Entitled To, and Should Demand

Cost-of-borrowing disclosure is not optional, and knowing what you are owed turns a vague conversation into a comparable number.

Before you sign, a lender should put four figures in front of you in writing:

1. **The APR** — the annual percentage rate, which folds mandatory fees into the rate so two offers can actually be compared.
2. **The total cost of borrowing in dollars** — what the credit costs you over the full term, expressed as money rather than a percentage.
3. **The payment amount and schedule** — how much, how often, for how many periods.
4. **Prepayment terms** — whether you may pay early or make lump sums, and whether anything is charged for doing so.

The second figure is the one that changes decisions. "35% APR over 48 months" is abstract; "$4,353 of interest on $5,000 borrowed" is not. Ask for it in that form, and ask for it **before** any optional insurance is added, so you can see what the insurance actually costs.

Two practical tests while you have the paperwork:

- **Does the total match the arithmetic?** Payment × number of payments should equal the total repayable. If it does not, something undisclosed is in there.
- **Is optional insurance pre-ticked?** If so, ask for the quote again without it. On a $5000 personal loan bad credit Canada lenders approve, an insurance premium bundled into the balance also accrues interest at the loan rate.

Getting these four numbers in writing costs nothing and is the single most effective protection available to you at this amount.
