## What the US government's complaint actually says

In **November 2024** the Federal Trade Commission filed a complaint against Dave Inc. in the US District Court for the Central District of California. The FTC then referred it to the Department of Justice, which filed an **amended complaint on December 30, 2024** naming both the company and its chief executive.

The allegations were specific:

- **Advertised amounts.** That Dave deceptively advertised the size of advances consumers could get.
- **The Express Fee.** That despite promising "instant" access, Dave charged a fee of **$3 to $25** for instant delivery that was **not disclosed until after sign-up was complete and the user had granted access to their bank accounts** — with a two-to-three business day wait as the alternative.
- **The "tip."** That consumers were often charged **15% of the advance** described as a tip, with many unaware they were being charged or that it could be avoided.
- **The charity claim.** That the app represented tips as funding meals for children in need while the company **kept the vast majority** and donated only a nominal sum.
- **Cancellation.** That recurring monthly fees were charged **without a simple mechanism to cancel** them, contrary to the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act.

These are allegations, not findings. Dave moved to dismiss in **February 2025**, the DOJ opposed in April 2025, and the matter remained in active litigation through 2026 without a settlement. Dave separately announced on **February 20, 2025** that it had completed a move to a **simplified 5% fee with a $5 minimum and a $15 cap**, removing tips and express fees entirely.

That is precisely why the complaint is useful. It isn't a verdict on any company. It is a public, government-drafted list of **where cost hides in this product category** — and Canadian apps use the same architecture.

## The four-point audit

Turn each allegation into a question and ask it of any app before you connect your bank account. These four tests are what separate the best cash advance apps like Dave Canada has from the ones that merely look the part.

**1. When is the express fee disclosed?** Not whether one exists — *when* you see it. If the number only appears after you've linked your account, you've already given up your leverage. Ask before you connect.

**2. Is the tip pre-selected, and can you set it to zero without friction?** A default is not a choice. California's financial regulator collected transaction data across this product type and found the **average tip was $4.09** where one was given — on advances that were **80% between $40 and $100**.

**3. What is the subscription, and how do you cancel it?** Find the cancellation path *before* you subscribe. If it isn't two taps inside the app, treat the monthly fee as permanent when you price the product.

**4. What does the whole thing cost annualised, at your actual advance size?** This is the one that changes decisions.

The minimum makes small advances expensive; the cap makes large ones cheap. The same holds for the $5 flat fee Canadian apps charge.

Set that against what the only regulator to gather real data found: an **average annual APR of 334% at tip-based providers and 331% at non-tip providers**, with an **average repayment time of 10 days**. ## Why a US app cannot simply onboard you

It is worth understanding *why* these apps are closed to Canadians, because it explains why the workarounds circulating online fail expensively rather than harmlessly.

![Checking eligibility before signing up for the best cash advance apps like Dave Canada searches return](https://images.pexels.com/photos/5717969/pexels-photo-5717969.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

**Identity.** US cash advance apps verify against a **Social Security number or ITIN**. A Canadian Social Insurance Number is a different, nine-digit system that their verification vendors cannot check.

**Address.** Dave's stated eligibility requires a **US physical or military address**. This is not decoration — state-by-state lending rules mean the address determines which product you can legally be offered, which is also why Dave excludes Colorado and Iowa.

**The money rails.** This is the hard blocker. US advances are funded and repaid over **ACH**, the American clearing system, using a routing number. A Canadian chequing account has an institution number and a transit number, not a routing number, and cannot sit on ACH. Canadian apps use **Interac e-Transfer** to fund and **pre-authorised debit** to repay — a completely separate set of rails.

So the workaround people try — a VPN plus a borrowed US address — cannot solve the third problem even when it defeats the first two. What it can do is get an account **frozen mid-cycle with an advance outstanding**, at which point you owe money to a company that will not talk to you, on rails your bank cannot reach, with no Canadian regulator to complain to.

## Before you borrow at all

- **Call the biller first.** Utilities, telecoms and municipalities run deferrals and equal-payment plans at no cost. A waived late fee beats every app in this article.
- **Know your NSF exposure on both sides.** Federally regulated banks have been limited to a **$10** NSF fee per returned transaction since **March 12, 2026** — most articles still quote $45–$48. The provider's own returned-payment fee is separate and is now usually the larger half.
- **Don't stack.** These apps cannot see each other. Three can advance against the same pay date, and all three debits land the same morning.
- **Take standard delivery once.** If you can survive the 1–3 day wait even occasionally, the free tier is genuinely free and the express fee is the entire price.
