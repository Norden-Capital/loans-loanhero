If you manage your money through a fintech app instead of a big-five bank, the question is refreshingly simple: **loans that accept KOHO in Canada** do exist, and more lenders can pay into a KOHO account than you might expect. Because a KOHO account has its own real account and transit numbers, it can receive a direct deposit just like a chequing account at a chartered bank. But there is a catch worth knowing before you apply, and this LoanHero guide breaks it down so you can pick a lender that actually works with your setup — no wasted applications, no surprise rejections.

## Quick Answer

Yes — you can get a loan deposited into a KOHO account. A KOHO spending account comes with a genuine account number, transit number and institution number, so most lenders that pay by direct deposit or Interac e-Transfer are technically able to send funds there. The friction is not the deposit itself; it is **verification**. Many lenders confirm your income by connecting to your bank automatically, and those tools do not always link to KOHO. When that happens, your best move is to ask for **manual verification** (uploading statements and a void cheque) or choose a lender that funds fintech accounts. Confirm the lender's policy before applying, and you will save yourself a needless credit inquiry.

## What KOHO Is — and What It Isn't

KOHO is a popular Canadian fintech that pairs a reloadable prepaid Mastercard with a spending-and-savings account. You can load it, spend with tap-to-pay, send and receive Interac e-Transfers, set money aside, and even build credit history through its optional credit-building product. For a lot of Canadians it functions as their everyday account.

Here is the important nuance: **KOHO is not a chartered bank.** It is a financial technology company, and the money in your account is held in trust with a regulated, CDIC-member partner rather than by KOHO itself. That structure is perfectly legitimate and increasingly common — but it is why some traditional lenders treat a KOHO account differently from a chequing account at RBC, TD or Scotiabank. You can read the specifics straight from [KOHO's official site](https://www.koho.ca/) if you want the current product details.

Crucially, your KOHO spending account has three things that make it usable for borrowing:

- **An account number** — unique to you.
- **A transit (branch) number** — pointing to the partner institution.
- **An institution number** — identifying the financial institution.

Those three numbers are exactly what a lender needs to send you a direct deposit. In plain terms, KOHO can *receive* money the same way a bank account can. That is the foundation for every loan that accepts KOHO.

## Why Your Account Type Matters to Lenders

If KOHO can receive deposits like any account, why do some lenders still say no? It comes down to how modern lenders check you out before releasing cash.

Most online lenders in Canada use **instant bank verification (IBV)** — a read-only connection to your bank, powered by an aggregator like Flinks, Plaid or Inverite. You log in through the tool, and the lender gets a quick, secure snapshot of your income and spending to confirm you can repay. It is fast, and it is why some lenders can approve you in minutes.

The problem: those aggregators do not connect to every institution, and **KOHO is not always supported.** If the lender's IBV tool cannot open your KOHO account, it cannot confirm your income automatically. Faced with that, a lender will do one of three things: decline, ask you to link a different account, or fall back to **manual verification** using documents you provide. On top of that, a minority of lenders simply have a policy of funding only major-bank accounts to streamline their pre-authorized debit collection. None of this means a KOHO loan is impossible — it just means you want a lender whose process fits your account.

## How to Get Approved With a KOHO Account

You can dramatically improve your odds with a little preparation. Here is the LoanHero game plan.

**1. Grab your KOHO deposit details.** Open the KOHO app and find your account, transit and institution numbers. You will need these to set up the direct deposit for your loan. Enter them exactly — one transposed digit is the most common reason a deposit bounces.

**2. Ask the lender two questions before applying.** First: "Do you fund fintech or prepaid accounts like KOHO?" Second: "If your bank-connection tool can't link to KOHO, do you offer manual income verification?" A straight answer to those two questions tells you whether it is worth submitting an application at all.

**3. Prepare your manual-verification kit.** If automated verification is off the table, most lenders will accept documents instead. Have these ready as clean PDFs:

- **60–90 days of bank statements** showing your income landing consistently.
- **Recent pay stubs** (or benefit/pension statements if that is your income).
- **A void cheque or a pre-authorized debit form** with your KOHO account details.
- Sometimes a **Notice of Assessment** or an **employment letter** for larger amounts.

Our guide to [preparing for a loan application](/resources/guides/preparing-for-loan-application) covers exactly how to assemble these so a reviewer can approve you quickly.

**4. Keep your KOHO account tidy.** Whether a lender reads it automatically or by eye, a clean account helps: steady deposits, few or no NSF (non-sufficient funds) events, and a positive balance around payday all signal that you can handle repayment.

When you have your documents in order and a lender that funds fintech accounts, you can [start an application](/loan-application) with confidence instead of crossing your fingers.

## The Trade-Offs and Risks

Being honest is part of being on your side, so here is the fine print.

- **Fewer lenders, sometimes higher cost.** The pool of lenders that fund KOHO — especially those offering manual verification — is smaller, and niche lenders can charge more. Compare the total cost, not just the monthly payment.
- **Manual verification is slower.** A human reviewing your statements takes longer than an instant connection. If you need money the same day, that delay matters.
- **The regulation you're protected by.** Payday loans in Canada are regulated province by province, with maximum charges typically in the range of **$14 to $17 per $100 borrowed**. Other loans, including installment loans, are capped at a **35% APR** federal criminal-interest limit that took effect January 1, 2025. If any offer blows past those limits, walk away.
- **Scam red flags.** Be extremely wary of "guaranteed approval," "no credit check for everyone," or any lender that asks you to **pay a fee upfront** to release your loan. Legitimate lenders deduct costs from the loan or your repayments — they do not demand cash in advance. The federal government's [payday loans resource](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/loans/payday-loans.html) is a solid plain-language reference, and our guide on [avoiding loan scams](/resources/guides/avoiding-loan-scams) shows how to spot the traps.

The reassuring headline is that **loans that accept KOHO in Canada** are real and more available than the rumours suggest — your KOHO account has genuine account and transit numbers, so it can receive a direct deposit like any chequing account.
