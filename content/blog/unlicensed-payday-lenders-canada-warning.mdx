Every **unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning** you can find online says the same thing: don't borrow from them. Sound advice, and completely useless to the person searching at midnight because they already did. This article is written for that person. The central fact almost nobody publishes is that an unlicensed lender has weakened its own legal position far more than yours — and in Ontario, the law says you may owe the money you received and nothing more.

## The unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning nobody publishes

Section 6(3) of Ontario's *Payday Loans Act, 2008* reads:

> "If a lender who is not licensed enters into a payday loan agreement with a borrower, the borrower is only required to repay the advance to the lender and is not liable to pay the cost of borrowing."

Read that twice. **The advance** is the money that landed in your account. **The cost of borrowing** is everything else — the fee, the charges, the extras. If the lender was not licensed, the second category is not something you are liable for.

The same remedial formula appears elsewhere in the Act for other breaches, and the other provinces with payday regimes have comparable provisions. If you are outside Ontario, confirm the specific wording where you live — but the pattern across Canadian payday legislation is consistent: licensing is the condition of being allowed to charge for the loan at all.

This reframes the whole situation. The real unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning is not that they are a scarier version of a licensed lender — it is that they gave up the right to charge you.

## The second layer: the Criminal Code

There is a federal dimension that makes an unlicensed operator's position weaker still.

Section 347 of the Criminal Code makes it an offence to charge above the **criminal rate of interest**, which fell to **35% APR on January 1, 2025**. Payday loans are carved out of that ceiling — but only under specific conditions: the loan must be **$1,500 or less**, for a term of **62 days or less**, made by a **provincially licensed lender**, in a **province designated** by the federal government as having a payday regime.

Strip out the licence and the carve-out disappears. What remains is a short-term loan measured against the 35% APR ceiling.

The arithmetic is unkind to the lender, and it is the part of any unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning worth understanding properly. The maximum legal payday cost of **$14 per $100** over a 14-day term annualizes to roughly **365% APR** — about ten times the criminal rate. An unlicensed operator charging typical payday pricing is not slightly offside. It is charging roughly ten times a ceiling that carries criminal consequences.

The same point applies geographically. **Quebec and the three territories have no designated payday regime.** A payday-priced loan made to a resident there never had the exemption in the first place.

## 7 rights that turn an unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning into action

### 1. The right to owe only what you received

Start here. Work out the **advance** — the exact figure deposited to your account — and separate it from every fee, charge and add-on. Under Ontario's s.6(3), that second column is what you are not liable for.

Do not concede the fees in a phone call before you have checked the lender's licence status. A demand is not the same as an entitlement.

### 2. The right to verify the licence, at any time

Search your **own province's** registry for the lender's **legal entity name**, not its brand — registries list corporations, and a brand-name mismatch is normal rather than suspicious. The legal name appears in a compliant site's footer, Terms of Use or Privacy Policy.

Then read the **status** field. "Expired," "Suspended," "Revoked" or no record at all is the finding that everything else follows from. If you need the full method, our [companion verification guide](/blog/is-my-canada-payday-legit) walks through it province by province.

### 3. The right to stop the withdrawals

Two separate actions, and doing only one is the common mistake.

**Revoke the pre-authorized debit** with the lender in writing — Payments Canada's Rule H1 contemplates at least 30 days' notice unless the agreement sets out another procedure. **Then place a stop payment** with your bank, which blocks the specific debit if it is attempted anyway. Expect roughly $12.50 to $30 depending on your bank and account.

You may also be entitled to **reimbursement from your bank** if a debit was not drawn in accordance with the agreement or was taken after you revoked authorization. That claim requires a signed declaration filed **within 10 business days** of the disputed debit — a short window, so check your account on the day a payment is due.

### 4. The right to be free of prohibited collection conduct

Provincial rules bind collectors regardless of whether the lender bothered to get licensed. In Ontario a payday lender **cannot contact you more than three times in a week**, **cannot contact your family, friends or acquaintances**, cannot repeatedly re-present a debit in a way that generates overdraft charges, and cannot use **threatening or intimidating language**.

Unlicensed operators break these rules routinely, because the whole business model already sits outside the system. Every breach is evidence. Keep a log with dates, times, numbers and what was said.

### 5. The right not to be bluffed about enforcement

Common threats, and what is actually true:

- **"We'll freeze your bank account tomorrow."** No lender can freeze an account by decision. That requires a court judgment and a separate order.
- **"We'll garnish your wages this week."** Garnishment follows a judgment. It is a matter of months, and it requires the lender to go to court — where it would have to explain lending without a licence.
- **"We'll tell your employer."** Contacting your workplace about a debt falls outside what provincial collection rules permit.
- **"This will destroy your credit score."** Most payday lenders do not report to Equifax or TransUnion at all. An unlicensed one is even less likely to have a reporting relationship. Consequences arrive through collections, not the credit file — our [payday loan default guide](/blog/payday-loan-default-consequences-canada) sets out the real sequence.

### 6. The right to complain to a regulator with teeth

Your **provincial consumer protection office** is the body that issues — and withholds — lending licences. It can investigate, order refunds, impose penalties and refer matters onward. That is a materially different proposition from a review site.

File with the agreement, e-transfer records, texts, emails and your call log attached.

### 7. The right to report without paying for the privilege

Reporting is free. Report to the **Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre** at **1-888-495-8501**, to your provincial office, and to your bank if unauthorized debits are landing.

And ignore anyone who contacts you afterwards offering to "recover" your money or "remove" you from lender lists for a fee. That is a second scam that targets people who have already been hit once.

## What to do this week: acting on an unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning

1. **Establish the licence position.** Search your provincial registry for the legal entity name and screenshot the result — including a "no results" page, which is itself the evidence.
2. **Do the arithmetic.** Write down the advance received, the total demanded, and the difference. That difference is the amount in dispute.
3. **Protect the account.** Revoke the pre-authorized debit in writing, then place a stop payment. Check whether any debit already taken falls inside the 10-business-day dispute window.
4. **Put your position in writing.** A short, calm email stating that you have confirmed the lender does not hold a licence in your province, that you are prepared to repay the advance, and that you dispute liability for the cost of borrowing. No hostility — this is a document you may hand to a regulator.
5. **Start the log.** Every call, text and email, with dates and times.
6. **File the complaint.** Provincial consumer protection office first, Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre second.
7. **Get advice if the amount is significant.** Community legal clinics, provincial consumer offices and services such as Steps to Justice can help without cost.

## How to avoid the next one

Two checks, ninety seconds, and they filter almost every operator an unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning is aimed at.

**Money must reach you before it leaves you.** Licensed lenders cannot request or require payment of any part of the cost of borrowing before the advance. Any upfront fee — processing, insurance, activation, a "good faith deposit" — is disqualifying, whatever the label, and requests for gift cards, cryptocurrency or wire transfers make fraud near-certain.

**Verify through a channel the sender does not control.** Sophisticated operators clone licensed lenders, including real licence numbers, so matching details prove nothing. Look the company up in the registry and use the contact details from that record — not the ones in the message that reached you.

Add the price check: since January 1, 2025 the federal cap has been **$14 per $100** in every licensed province, replacing the older $15 and $17 provincial caps. A quote above that is a lender outside the system.

## The bottom line

The most useful unlicensed payday lenders Canada warning is not "avoid them." It is that a lender operating without a licence has forfeited the legal machinery it needs to charge you — in Ontario, section 6(3) means you repay the advance and are not liable for the cost of borrowing, and without a licence there is no Criminal Code exemption from the 35% APR ceiling either.
