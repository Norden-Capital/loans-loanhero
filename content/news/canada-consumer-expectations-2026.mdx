**Canada consumer expectations 2026** point to a wary, budget-tightening household: Canadians expect higher inflation over the year ahead and are pulling back on spending, according to the Bank of Canada's latest **Canadian Survey of Consumer Expectations**. Released July 6, 2026, and run from April 27 to May 27, the second-quarter survey found near-term inflation expectations still elevated amid trade tensions and rising energy-price worries tied to the Middle East conflict. For anyone carrying high-interest debt, the practical takeaway is clear: when consumers brace for higher prices, protecting monthly cash flow matters more than ever.

## What the Bank of Canada Survey Found

The headline is that inflation psychology has not cooled. The Bank of Canada reports that a slightly larger share of consumers than in the prior quarter expect inflation to run above **3%** over the next 12 months. Longer-horizon views also drifted higher, with two- and five-year-ahead expectations edging up.

The *why* is shifting. Tariffs remained the most cited inflation driver, but mentions of energy prices **rose sharply** from the previous quarter — a change the Bank links to the Middle East conflict and its knock-on effect on oil and gasoline costs. In short, Canadians are watching the pump again, and it is reshaping how they think about the year ahead.

Here is how the second-quarter readings moved compared with the previous survey:

| Survey measure (Q2 2026) | Direction vs. prior quarter |
|---|---|
| Share expecting inflation above 3% (next 12 months) | Slightly higher |
| Two- and five-year inflation expectations | Edged up |
| Energy prices cited as an inflation driver | Rose sharply |
| Tariffs as the top cited driver | Still #1 |
| Consumer spending plans | Still held back |
| Labour-market index (job-loss worries) | Edged up as worries eased |

## Why Cautious Consumers Are Spending Less

Elevated price expectations translate into defensive behaviour at the till. The Bank of Canada notes that concerns about high prices and economic uncertainty are **still holding back consumer spending plans**. The effect is sharpest among a specific group: households that expect the Middle East conflict to significantly raise inflation.

Those households told the survey they are more likely to:

- **Substitute cheaper essentials** — trading down on groceries and everyday items.
- **Cut discretionary spending** — trimming the nice-to-haves first.
- **Drive less** — a direct response to the fear of higher gasoline prices.

It was not all defensive, though. The labour-market index edged up as job-loss worries eased, particularly in trade-sensitive sectors that had felt most exposed to tariff uncertainty. And a one-time top-up to the **Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit** appeared to support spending for some households — a reminder that targeted relief can move the needle when budgets are stretched.
