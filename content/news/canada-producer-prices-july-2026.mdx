## Brief summary

**Canada producer prices** rose **0.6%** in July and **12.4%** compared with a year earlier, Statistics Canada reported on August 20, 2026. Strip out energy and petroleum products, however, and the Industrial Product Price Index **fell 0.2%** on the month. The entire increase came from one place: refined fuel.

![Canada producer prices — a manufacturing worker on a factory floor, where the Industrial Product Price Index is measured at the factory gate](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6237888/pexels-photo-6237888.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Introduction

Statistics Canada released the July figures for the Industrial Product Price Index (IPPI) and the Raw Materials Price Index (RMPI) on Thursday. The headline numbers look alarming — a 12.4% annual rise in what Canadian factories charge, four times the 3.0% consumer inflation rate reported three days earlier.

The composition tells a much narrower story, and it is the composition that matters to households.

## The main story: what moved Canada producer prices in July

The IPPI rose **0.6% month over month**, its **22nd consecutive month of year-over-year increase**. But that gain was carried entirely by energy.

- **Energy and petroleum products: +6.4%** on the month
- **Refined petroleum energy products: +6.9%**
- **Diesel fuel: +10.4%**
- **Finished motor gasoline: +4.7%**
- **IPPI excluding energy and petroleum products: −0.2%**

That last line is the release's real finding. Outside fuel, Canadian factory-gate prices went *down*.

Several categories fell sharply:

| Category | Month-over-month change |
|---|---|
| Primary non-ferrous metals | **−5.2%** |
| Aluminum alloys | **−7.0%** (largest drop since April 2025) |
| Chemicals and chemical products | **−3.4%** |
| Fertilizers and pesticides | **−10.8%** |

Over 12 months the standouts were **energy and petroleum products (+42.0%)**, **unwrought silver (+60.3%)** and **plastic resins (+46.3%)** — commodity and export categories rather than consumer goods.

## Background: Canada producer prices rose as raw materials fell

The second index in the same release moved in the opposite direction, which is unusual and worth noting.

The **Raw Materials Price Index fell 2.2%** in July — that is what Canadian producers *pay* for inputs — while the IPPI, what they *charge*, rose 0.6%. Within the RMPI:

- Crude energy products: **−3.6%**
- Metal ores and concentrates: **−4.7%**
- Gold, silver and platinum ores: **−7.2%**
- Excluding crude energy products: **−1.5%** on the month, **+16.7%** year over year

Year over year the RMPI was still up **18.1%**.

So input costs eased in July while output prices rose. The gap sits almost entirely in refining margins on fuel.

## Why Canada producer prices matter to households

Producer prices are a pipeline indicator, not a bill. They do not pass through to consumers one for one, and a large share of the IPPI's annual increase reflects globally priced commodities that Canadians export rather than buy.

Two things in this release do reach households.

**Fuel.** Diesel up 10.4% and gasoline up 4.7% in a single month is the part that travels. July's consumer inflation reading already showed gasoline up **25.7%** year over year, and this release suggests that pressure had not finished working through in July.

**Freight.** Diesel is the input that moves nearly everything else. When it jumps in one month, the effect shows up later in delivered goods rather than immediately at the pump.

Everything else in the release points the other way. Falling metals, falling fertilizer and falling ore prices are disinflationary signals sitting in the pipeline behind the headline.

## Key takeaways

- **Canada producer prices** rose **0.6%** in July 2026 and **12.4%** year over year — the 22nd straight annual increase.
- Excluding energy and petroleum, the index **fell 0.2%**. The monthly gain was entirely fuel.
- **Diesel rose 10.4%** and finished motor gasoline **4.7%** in one month.
- The **Raw Materials Price Index fell 2.2%**, so input costs eased while output prices rose.
- Metals, chemicals and fertilizers all declined, with **aluminum alloys down 7.0%** — the largest fall since April 2025.
- A 12.4% producer reading does **not** mean 12.4% consumer inflation; July CPI came in at **3.0%**.

## Conclusion

The headline on **Canada producer prices** overstates what households will feel. One input did all the work, and the index that measures what producers pay for their materials actually fell.

For borrowers watching the Bank of Canada, the useful read is that this release contains both an inflationary signal and a disinflationary one, and they are not the same size. Fuel is the pressure; metals, chemicals and fertilizer are the offset. The Bank's next scheduled interest rate decision is **September 2, 2026**.
