**TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026** data released on July 22 describes a country pulling in two directions at once. Optimism is up — 45% of Canadians expect their household finances to improve over the next 12 months, and 24% say finances have turned out better than expected this year, the highest reading in a year. Yet **50% still say their income is not keeping pace with inflation**, and 86% rank inflation among their top three financial concerns. The recovery is real. It is also unevenly distributed.

## What the TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 study found

The headline tension is between sentiment and arithmetic. Optimism at 45% and the "better than expected" reading at 24% both point upward. Meanwhile, one in four Canadians (25%) reported household income rising in the past three months — an improvement, but a minority experience.

"Many Canadians are beginning to see improvements in their financial outlook," said **Matt Fabian, senior director of financial services research and consulting at TransUnion Canada**, adding that "affordability continues to shape everyday financial decisions."

That shaping is visible in behaviour. Just over half of Canadians (51%) cut discretionary spending on dining, travel and entertainment, and 26% cancelled subscriptions or memberships outright. Eighteen percent accelerated debt repayment.

But 11% **increased** discretionary spending. Households are not moving as a bloc; they are separating into those whose finances have stabilised and those still compressing.

## Borrowing intentions are shifting

The TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 borrowing data is where sentiment turns into action. A quarter of Canadians (25%) plan to apply for new credit or refinance existing credit. Among that group, **49% are targeting credit cards** — the most expensive mainstream revolving product, and a notable choice in a year when affordability is the dominant concern.

Equally telling is the credit that never gets applied for. **21% considered applying and decided not to**, and 26% of them cited borrowing costs as the reason. That is demand suppressed by price rather than by lack of need — a pattern that tends to push households toward smaller, faster, higher-cost alternatives when a genuine gap appears.

## Background: the data around the survey

The TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 findings sit between two official releases that broadly corroborate them.

- **July 15, 2026** — The Bank of Canada held its policy rate at 2¼%, keeping prime at 4.45%.
- **July 22, 2026** — TransUnion publishes the Q2 2026 Consumer Pulse study.
- **July 29, 2026** — Bank of Canada deliberations show Governing Council agreed inflation is easing, projecting about 2½% in the second half of 2026, but split on whether the rebound lasts beyond that.
- **July 30, 2026** — Statistics Canada reports average weekly earnings of $1,337.77 in May, up 3.4% year over year, with job vacancies flat at 495,700 for a fifth month.

Earnings rising 3.4% against inflation heading toward 2½% should, arithmetically, leave households ahead. Half of them report the opposite. The gap between the aggregate and the lived experience is the most useful thing in this data.

## Fraud exposure keeps climbing

The security section of the TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 study deserves separate attention. **44% of Canadians were targeted by fraud** without becoming victims, and 20% were notified that they were caught up in a data breach.

Canadians are responding, mostly by watching: **40% now check their credit report at least monthly**, up three percentage points year over year. But **33% took no cybersecurity action at all**, and of those, **51% said they were unsure what steps to take** — a knowledge gap rather than indifference.

Checking your credit report is free and does not affect your score. The [FCAC's guidance on ordering a credit report](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/credit-reports-score/order-credit-report.html) sets out how to request one from each bureau.

## Why this matters

For anyone weighing borrowing this year, three implications follow from the TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 findings.

**Optimism is not a plan.** Improving sentiment is not the same as improving cash flow, and the Bank of Canada's own Governing Council is divided on how long current conditions hold.

**Credit cards are a costly default.** Half of prospective borrowers are heading there. For consolidating existing balances, a fixed-term [debt consolidation loan](/loans/by-type/debt-consolidation-loans) typically costs less over the same period than revolving a card balance.

**Small gaps deserve small solutions.** Where a shortfall is genuinely short-term, the total cost matters more than the label. A payday loan is capped federally at **$14 per $100** — roughly 365% annualised on a two-week term — and your real limit is 50% of one net paycheque (30% in Manitoba), not the advertised $1,500. ## Conclusion

The TransUnion Canada Consumer Pulse 2026 study captures a turning point that has not yet reached everyone. Aggregate conditions are improving — inflation easing, earnings rising, optimism recovering — while half the country reports its income still falling behind.

The practical response is unglamorous: check your credit report, cancel what you no longer use, and price any new borrowing in total dollars over the exact number of days you need it. Where balances have already accumulated, consolidating at a fixed rate beats revolving at a card rate.
