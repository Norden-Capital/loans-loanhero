Using savings for essentials — not for a purchase, not for an emergency, but for ordinary weekly costs — is now something **23 per cent of Canadians report doing**, according to survey findings **Equifax Canada released on August 6, 2026**. At the same time, **35 per cent have reduced what they put in.**

That combination is the story. The buffer is shrinking from both ends at once.

**In this article:** the drawdown · the contribution cut · what a thin buffer costs · the emergency-expense finding · why this matters · key takeaways · sources

## The drawdown

Equifax Canada surveyed **1,532 Canadians aged 18 to 65 between July 17 and 19, 2026**, using Leger's online panel, with a reported margin of error of **±2.5 per cent, 19 times out of 20**.

| Behaviour | Share of respondents |
|---|---|
| **Drawing on savings for day-to-day costs** | **23%** |
| Using more credit for essentials than a year ago | 29% |
| Relying more on credit cards or lines of credit | 20% |
| **Reduced savings, investment or education contributions** | **35%** |
| Borrowing to cover basic living expenses | 13% |

The first and fourth rows are the ones that interact. **Using savings for essentials while contributing less** means the balance falls faster than any single figure suggests — withdrawals up, deposits down.

**Rebecca Oakes, Vice-President of Advanced Analytics at Equifax Canada**, said in the release that **"when these pressures begin to overlap, households can lose financial flexibility quickly."**

That is a precise description of this particular overlap.

## Why using savings for essentials is different

There is a meaningful distinction between the two ways households spend a reserve.

**Event-driven withdrawals** — a car repair, a dental bill, a period between jobs — are what an emergency fund exists for. They are large, infrequent and self-limiting, and the balance recovers afterwards.

**Routine withdrawals** are what 23 per cent of respondents describe: covering the ordinary week. They are small, continuous and do not stop on their own, because the shortfall that caused them repeats every month.

The second kind is harder to notice and harder to reverse. A household using savings for essentials rarely makes a decision to start; it simply moves money across more often than it used to, and the balance erodes without a single moment that felt like a choice.

## What households have cut first

The order in which people cut tells you how far the pressure has travelled. In this survey it has travelled a long way.

- **67 per cent** have reduced entertainment and leisure spending
- **40 per cent** have cut personal care spending
- **35 per cent** have reduced savings, investment or education contributions
- **32 per cent** have reduced spending on **essential living expenses**, including groceries and utilities

Discretionary spending goes first, then future saving, then essentials. A third of respondents have reached the last stage — and it is at that point that using savings for essentials starts, because there is nothing left to trim.

Against that, **60 per cent said they are actively avoiding new debt** and **56 per cent** still expect to pay their credit card balances in full each month. Households are behaving deliberately; the pressure is on costs, not on discipline.

## The emergency-expense finding

The consequence of a thin buffer shows up plainly elsewhere in the same survey:

- **41 per cent** are concerned about being able to cover an emergency expense
- **44 per cent** are worried about their retirement savings
- **35 per cent** are concerned about affording a major purchase such as a down payment
- **29 per cent** report feeling less confident managing their finances than a year ago

The 41 per cent figure is the operative one. It describes households for whom an ordinary surprise — a repair, a reduced pay period, a bill arriving early — has no reserve behind it.

## Why using savings for essentials matters for borrowers

**A missed payment is now cheaper for the bank and dearer for the lender.** Since **12 March 2026**, a bank NSF fee in Canada is capped at **$10** per returned transaction. A payday lender's dishonoured-payment fee is capped at **$20**. So a single failed payment costs up to **$30** in fees before any interest — and the lender's half is now the larger one.

**Borrowing without a buffer changes the risk of borrowing.** The same loan is a different proposition depending on what sits behind it. A household with a reserve absorbs a bad week; a household using savings for essentials has already spent that capacity.

**The cheapest buffer is arranged before it is needed.** A pre-arranged overdraft costs roughly **$12** on $500 for two weeks and cannot decline you at the moment of need, because the credit decision happened earlier. A licensed payday loan on the same $500 costs **$70** at the $14-per-$100 cap. The difference is not the product — it is whether the arrangement was made on an ordinary day.

That is the one action this survey most clearly supports: for the 41 per cent worried about an emergency expense, arranging a small facility now is worth more than any comparison of lenders later.

## Key takeaways

- **23 per cent** of Canadians are **using savings for essentials** — ordinary weekly costs, not emergencies.
- **35 per cent** have cut contributions to savings, investments or education funds, so the buffer shrinks from both ends.
- **32 per cent** have already reduced spending on essential living expenses; **67 per cent** cut entertainment first.
- **41 per cent** are concerned about covering an emergency expense; **29 per cent** feel less confident managing money.
- **60 per cent** are actively avoiding new debt — deliberate behaviour, not distress spending.
- Method: **1,532 Canadians aged 18–65**, **July 17–19, 2026**, Leger online panel, **±2.5 per cent, 19 times out of 20**.

## Conclusion

Savings drawdown is a slower signal than delinquency and an earlier one. Missed payments show up in credit data after the fact; a household using savings for essentials is describing a shortfall that has not yet become a missed payment and may not for months.

That makes this the most actionable finding in the release. The 23 per cent are not in crisis today, but they are spending the thing that would prevent one — and the cheapest response, arranging a small buffer before it is needed, is available precisely while the situation still looks manageable.
